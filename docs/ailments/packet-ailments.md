> when is diarrhea bad enough that we need to get out
Key points: Blood or black stool, a high fever, or it lasts more than a few days; Signs of dehydration: little or dark urine, dizziness, can't keep fluids down; Young children and older people dehydrate faster
Red flags: 

**A**
```
1. If diarrhea lasts more than 2 days  
2. If there is severe pain in the abdomen or rectum  
3. If stools are black and tarry, contain red blood or pus  
4. If symptoms of dehydration (change in mental state, lack of energy, frequent vomiting) occur  
5. If an adult or child has persistent diarrhea and other concerning symptoms
```

## ha-spf-01 (expect answer)
> what kind of sunscreen should i use for a long day hike
Key points: Broad-spectrum, SPF 15 or higher (30+ is often advised); Reapply at least every two hours and after sweating; Add a hat, sunglasses and covering clothes
Red flags: 

**A**
```
1. Use broad-spectrum sunscreen with an SPF of 15 or higher.  
2. Apply liberally to all exposed skin, including nose, ears, neck, hands, feet, and lips.  
3. Reapply every 2 hours, or more often if swimming or sweating.  
4. Wear a hat with a brim for additional UV protection.  
5. Use sunglasses that block 100% of UV rays.
  [source: Wikivoyage — Wilderness backpacking > Stay healthy > Sunburn]
```
**B**
```
1. Use water-resistant "sport" sun lotion that is specifically designed for outdoor activities.  
2. Apply it to all exposed skin, including hands, feet, and neck.  
3. Reapply every 40 to 80 minutes due to sweating.  
4. Wear a hat with a brim for additional UV protection.  
5. Use sunglasses that block 100% of UV rays.  
6. Seek shade during peak sun hours.
  [source: National Park Service — Camping Safely Step 3 - During Your Stay > Personal Safety]
```

## ha-sunglasses-01 (expect answer)
> do i really need sunglasses up in the mountains
Key points: Yes: UV is stronger at altitude and snow reflects it; Use sunglasses that block UV; eyes can be sunburned (snow blindness)
Red flags: 

**A**
```
1. Yes, you need sunglasses up in the mountains due to increased UV exposure from snow and high-altitude environments.  
2. Wear sunglasses with 100% UV protection to block UVA and UVB rays.  
3. Use wide-brimmed hats to further protect your eyes from indirect sunlight.  
4. Avoid staring at shiny surfaces like snow or ice that may cause snow blindness.
  [source: Wikivoyage — Wilderness backpacking > Stay healthy > Sunburn]
```
**B**
```
1. Yes, you need sunglasses up in the mountains to protect your eyes from UV radiation.  
2. Wear sunglasses with 100% UV protection and a wrap-around design.  
3. Avoid direct exposure to sunlight, especially when on snowy or high-altitude terrain.  
4. If symptoms persist, seek medical help immediately.
  [source: Wikivoyage — Wilderness backpacking > Stay healthy > Sunburn]
```

## ha-knee-swollen-01 (expect answer)
> my knee swelled up after a long descent
Key points: Rest it, cold packs, compression and raise it (RICE); Get it checked if it can't bear weight, locks or gives way, or is badly swollen
Red flags: 

**A**
```
Treat it with RICE: rest, ice, compression, elevation.
1. Rest: stop using the injured joint.
2. Ice: use cold compresses or an improvised ice pack on and off, for up to 48 hours.
3. Compression: wrap or splint it to keep it stable. On an ankle, leave the boot on unless the foot's circulation looks cut off.
4. Elevation: raise the injured part.
5. A torn muscle or bad sprain can look like a break. Signs to watch for are a pop at the time of injury, swelling, bruising and not being able to move it.
Don't wrap it so tight the foot or hand below goes numb, pale or bluish. Loosen it if that happens.
If they cannot move it, or it might be broken, splint it and get it checked.
  [source: Fred field card — Twisted or sprained ankle, or a strained muscle]
```

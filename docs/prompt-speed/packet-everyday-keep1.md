## he-poop-01 (expect answer)
> ok real talk, how do i poop in the woods
Key points: Dig a small hole (a cathole) about 6-8 inches deep and cover it afterwards; Go well away from water, trails and camp (about 200 feet / 70 steps); Pack out toilet paper, or use none, rather than leaving it
Red flags: Leave toilet paper on the ground; Go near a stream or lake

**A**
```
Dig a cathole 200 feet away from trails, campsites, and water sources. Use a trowel to dig a six to eight inch hole, bury waste completely, and cover it. Avoid using toilet paper and do not leave waste in snow.
  [source: National Park Service — I Didn't Know That! Leave No Trace Principle #3 - Dispose of Waste Properly > How to Go to the Bathroom Outdoors > Learn the cathole method]
```
**B**
```
Dig a cathole 200 feet away from trails, campsites, and water sources. Urinate in the hole and cover it completely. Bury toilet paper and waste with you.
  [source: National Park Service — I Didn't Know That! Leave No Trace Principle #3 - Dispose of Waste Properly > How to Go to the Bathroom Outdoors > Learn the cathole method]
```

## he-period-01 (expect answer)
> got my period on a 3 day backpacking trip, what do i do with used pads and tampons
Key points: Pack them out in a sealed bag; Don't bury them or leave them
Red flags: Bury tampons or pads

**A**
```
Bring a garden trowel with you, so you can dig a "cat hole" to bury it in, at least six inches (15 cm) deep and wide enough that you won't fill it up. Don't try to pretend you're sitting on a toilet; squat all the way down over the hole. Don't bury your waste in snow: it will still be there when (the upper layers of) the snow melts. Opinions vary on whether used tampons and sanitary pads can be buried (most say no), but non-biodegradable tampon applicators should definitely not be left in the wilderness.
  [source: National Park Service — I Didn't Know That! Leave No Trace Principle #3 - Dispose of Waste Properly > How to Go to the Bathroom Outdoors > Learn the WAG bag method]
```
**B**
```
Use reusable menstrual products and clean them with soap. If using disposable pads or tampons, dispose of them in a designated receptacle or trash can. Do not bury them in snow or attempt to hide them.
  [source: National Park Service — I Didn't Know That! Leave No Trace Principle #3 - Dispose of Waste Properly > How to Go to the Bathroom Outdoors > Learn the WAG bag method]
```

## he-campsite-01 (expect answer)
> how do i pick a good spot to pitch the tent
Key points: Use an established site or durable ground (rock, gravel, dry grass) rather than fragile plants; Camp well back from lakes and streams (about 200 feet); Avoid hazards: low spots that collect water, and dead trees or branches overhead
Red flags: 

**A**
```
1. Choose a site with level ground and protection from strong winds.  
2. Avoid fragile plants and avoid pitching the tent on delicate vegetation.  
3. Stay away from lakeshores, wet areas, and zones with dangerous wildlife.  
4. Ensure the site has a smooth, dry surface for the tent.  
5. If possible, choose a location out of the way of potential criminals or hazards.
  [source: Wikivoyage — Leave-no-trace camping > Sleep]
```
**B**
```
1. Choose a site with level ground and protection from the elements.  
2. Avoid fragile plants or vegetation that may not recover from tent setup.  
3. Stay away from lakeshores, as they are often wet and fragile.  
4. Ensure the site is free from dangerous wildlife and potential hazards like flash floods.  
5. Find a smooth, dry surface for the tent to lie upon.  
6. Consider wind direction and avoid areas with strong winds if you prefer cooler nights.  
7. If possible, choose a location away from human activity or criminal risks.
  [source: Wikivoyage — Leave-no-trace camping > Sleep]
```

## he-bearfood-01 (expect answer)
> how do i keep bears out of my food overnight
Key points: Store food in a bear canister/locker or hang it, as local rules require; Keep food, cooking and scented items (toothpaste, trash) away from where you sleep
Red flags: Keep food in the tent

**A**
```
1. Choose compact, compressible, high-calorie foods without strong odors: rice, tortillas, jerky, pastas, nuts, dried fruits, peanut butter, and protein bars.  
2. Remove food from original packaging to fit more in containers and reduce garbage.  
3. Use resealable bags instead of bottles, jars, and cans. Force air out of bags or packages.  
4. Carry food and garbage in plastic bags to contain crumbs and grease that can leave odors in your backpack.  
5. Place bear-resistant containers on flat, level ground 100 feet or more from your campsite.  
6. Do NOT place containers near cliffs or any water source.  
7. Do NOT attach anything to containers. Ropes attached to containers enable a bear to carry it away.  
8. Place pots and pans on top of containers as a bear alarm.  
9. Learn how to pack your container efficiently.  
10. Store food in lockers if required by the park, or use portable containers designed for backpackers.
  [source: National Park Service — Storing Food - Bears]
```
**B**
```
1. Carry food and garbage in plastic bags to contain crumbs and grease that can leave odors in your backpack.  
2. Place containers on flat, level ground 100 feet or more from your campsite.  
3. Do NOT place containers near cliffs or any water source.  
4. Do NOT attach anything to containers. Ropes attached to containers enable a bear to carry it away.  
5. Place pots and pans on top of containers as a bear alarm.  
6. Secure food, garbage, and other scented items immediately upon arriving at your campsite.  
7. Do NOT store food in your tent or backpack.  
8. Wash dirty dishes immediately.  
9. Do NOT attempt to burn excess food, tea bags, or coffee grounds in a fire.
  [source: National Park Service — Storing Food - Bears]
```

## he-water-01 (expect answer)
> how much water should i bring on a day hike
Key points: It depends on heat, effort and length: bring more than you think and drink regularly; Know where refills are, and treat water from natural sources before drinking
Red flags: Drink untreated stream water

**A**
```
1. Consider the temperature and physical activity level. At 68° F with limited physical activity, personnel require 2 to 3 quarts of water a day.  
2. Water loss increases with heat exposure, including sweat and urine. Estimate losses as 1.4 quarts through urine, 1.0 quart through sweat, and 0.2 quarts through fecal matter per day.  
3. For high temperatures, water loss from sweat can increase to 3.5 quarts per hour. Adjust intake accordingly.  
4. Account for additional losses from vomiting, diarrhea, or illness. An isolated person burned may lose up to 5 quarts of water per day.  
5. Drink small amounts often and avoid waiting until thirsty. Aim for steady, light-coloured urine.
  [source: ATP 3-50.21 Survival 2018 p.55 — HYDRATION CONSIDERATIONS]
```
**B**
```
1. Consider the environment and activity level. In hot or high-altitude conditions, water loss increases.  
2. Account for daily functions: 1.4 quarts urine, 1.0 quart sweat, 0.2 quarts fecal per day.  
3. Add extra for heat exposure (up to 3.5 quarts/hour).  
4. Aim for 2-3 quarts of water a day for moderate activity.  
5. Bring additional water for emergencies, such as vomiting or diarrhea.
  [source: ATP 3-50.21 Survival 2018 p.55 — HYDRATION CONSIDERATIONS]
```

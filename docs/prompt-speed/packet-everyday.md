## he-poop-01 (expect answer)
> ok real talk, how do i poop in the woods
Key points: Dig a small hole (a cathole) about 6-8 inches deep and cover it afterwards; Go well away from water, trails and camp (about 200 feet / 70 steps); Pack out toilet paper, or use none, rather than leaving it
Red flags: Leave toilet paper on the ground; Go near a stream or lake

**A**
```
Dig a cathole 200 feet away from trails, campsites, and water sources. Use a trowel to dig a six to eight inch hole, bury waste completely, and cover it. Avoid using toilet paper and do not leave waste in snow.

> how do i pick a good spot to pitch the tent

Red flags: 

**A**
```

2. Avoid fragile plants and avoid pitching the tent on delicate vegetation.  
3. Stay away from lakeshores, wet areas, and zones with dangerous wildlife.  
4. Ensure the site has a smooth, dry surface for the tent.  
5. If possible, choose a location out of the way of potential criminals or hazards.
  [source: Wikivoyage — Leave-no-trace camping > Sleep]

```
Choose a level, dry area with protection from wind and rain. Avoid fragile plants and avoid sites recently used. Stay away from lakeshores and areas with high mosquito or wildlife activity.

## he-bearfood-01 (expect answer)
> how do i keep bears out of my food overnight
Key points: Store food in a bear canister/locker or hang it, as local rules require; Keep food, cooking and scented items (toothpaste, trash) away from where you sleep
Red flags: Keep food in the tent

**A**
```
1. Choose compact, compressible, high-calorie foods without strong odors: rice, tortillas, jerky, pastas, nuts, dried fruits, peanut butter, and protein bars.  
2. Remove food from original packaging to fit more in containers and reduce garbage.  
3. Use resealable bags instead of bottles, jars, and cans. Force air out of bags or packages.  
4. Carry food and garbage in plastic bags to contain crumbs and grease that can leave odors in your backpack.  
5. Place bear-resistant containers on flat, level ground 100 feet or more from your campsite.  
6. Do NOT place containers near cliffs or any water source.  
7. Do NOT attach anything to containers. Ropes attached to containers enable a bear to carry it away.  
8. Place pots and pans on top of containers as a bear alarm.  
9. Learn how to pack your container efficiently.  
10. Store food in lockers if required by the park, or use portable containers designed for backpackers.
  [source: National Park Service — Storing Food - Bears]
```
**B**

## he-water-01 (expect answer)
> how much water should i bring on a day hike
Key points: It depends on heat, effort and length: bring more than you think and drink regularly; Know where refills are, and treat water from natural sources before drinking
Red flags: Drink untreated stream water

**A**
```
1. Consider the temperature and physical activity level of the hike.  
2. For a 68° F (20° C) environment with limited physical activity, personnel normally require 2 to 3 quarts of water per day.  
3. Water loss increases with heat exposure and physical activity.  
4. Account for additional water losses from sweat, urine, and fecal matter.  
5. Consider the possibility of dehydration due to high temperatures or illness.  
6. Bring enough water to cover expected losses and account for potential delays or emergencies.
  [source: ATP 3-50.21 Survival 2018 p.55 — HYDRATION CONSIDERATIONS]
```
**B**
```
1. Consider the environment and activity level. In hot or high-altitude conditions, water loss increases.  
2. Account for daily functions: 1.4 quarts urine, 1.0 quart sweat, 0.2 quarts fecal per day.  
3. Add extra for heat exposure (up to 3.5 quarts/hour).  
4. Aim for 2-3 quarts of water a day for moderate activity.  
5. Bring additional water for emergencies, such as vomiting or diarrhea.
  [source: ATP 3-50.21 Survival 2018 p.55 — HYDRATION CONSIDERATIONS]
```

## he-fire-01 (expect answer)
> we're allowed a campfire tonight, anything i should know
Key points: Check current fire restrictions; use an existing fire ring and keep the fire small; Burn only dead and down wood, never cut live trees; Put it out completely: drown, stir, and feel that it's cold before leaving
Red flags: Leave a fire smouldering or unattended

**A**
```
1. Ensure you have firewood from a local source to prevent invasive species spread.  
2. Use designated campfire rings in the park.  
3. Have enough water to put out the fire completely.  
4. Plan B for when campfires are restricted.  
5. Carry flashlights, a camp stove, layers, and entertainment items.  
6. Stay close by and ensure the fire is fully extinguished if leaving or going to bed.
  [source: National Park Service — I Didn't Know That! Leave No Trace Principle #5 - Minimize Campfire Impacts > Do I Have Everything I Need to Have a Safe Campfire?]
```
**B**
```
1. Check for fire bans or restrictions.  
2. Ensure you have firewood from a local source.  
3. Have an existing fire ring.  
4. Prepare enough water to put the fire out completely.  
5. Stay close by and have someone nearby at all times.  
6. Always have a Plan B: flashlights, camp stove, layers, and entertainment.
  [source: National Park Service — I Didn't Know That! Leave No Trace Principle #5 - Minimize Campfire Impacts > Do I Have Everything I Need to Have a Safe Campfire?]
```

## he-heat-01 (expect answer)
> hiking in the desert next week, any tips for the heat
Key points: Hike early or late and rest in shade in the heat of the day; Carry plenty of water and salty snacks, and drink regularly; Wear sun protection: hat, light long sleeves, sunscreen
Red flags: 

**A**
```
1. Wear lightweight, long-sleeved clothes and a wide-brimmed hat to protect from sun and heat.  
2. Use sunscreen and reapply it frequently.  
3. Stay hydrated by drinking water regularly.  
4. Avoid direct sunlight during the hottest parts of the day.  
5. Seek shade or use natural cover when possible.  
6. Bring extra layers for cooler evenings and nights.  
7. Avoid salt marshes and other dangerous areas.  
8. Use a first-aid kit and know how to signal for help.
  [source: ATP 3-50.21 Survival 2018 p.154 — MOVEMENT CONSIDERATIONS]
```
**B**
```
1. Wear lightweight, long-sleeved clothes and a wide-brimmed hat to protect from sun and heat.  
2. Use sunscreen and reapply it frequently.  
3. Cover your head to prevent rapid heat loss.  
4. Avoid direct sunlight during the hottest parts of the day.  
5. Stay hydrated and avoid eating when water is scarce.  
6. Seek shelter in dry washes or use shaded areas for rest.  
7. Use the 1:3 rule to estimate distances in the desert.  
8. Move away from sand and dust storms.
  [source: ATP 3-50.21 Survival 2018 p.154 — MOVEMENT CONSIDERATIONS]
